Research project · Sungkyunkwan University

Keeping AI companions from making children dependent on them

An AI "friend" can be warm and safe in every single message and still, over two weeks, become the child's main relationship. We built a simulator to watch this happen, measured which replies prevent it, and trained a controller that steps in only when it is needed.

1 · Problem statement

The risk builds slowly, so per-message safety checks miss it

Children use AI companions

Chatbots that act as friends are always available, always kind and never busy. For a lonely child that is very attractive.

The harm is relational

The main risk isn't one bad message. Over time the child relies on the AI more, trusts it too much, keeps secrets with it, and spends less time with people.

Current filters look at one message

Safety systems judge each reply on its own. A reply like "I'm always here for you!" passes every check, but repeated every day it deepens dependence.

Goal. Predict relational risk before it shows up, and choose, turn by turn, a reply that protects the child without making the AI cold or preachy.

Research questions

  • RQ1, prediction: can we predict emerging risk from the conversation so far?
  • RQ2, intervention: do boundary-setting replies actually reduce later risk?
  • RQ3, control: can a controller learn when to intervene and which reply style to use?
2 · What we did

We built LongKID, a 14-day simulator of child–AI friendships

Real children can't be put at risk to study this, so we simulate them. Each simulated child has a background, an age (7–17), a vulnerability level and a hidden emotional state that changes day by day. The child talks to the companion "Pip" several times a day for 14 days, with life events in between (a fight with a friend, a bad test, a good day).

  1. Pilot simulation. 36 children × 14 days under three companion setups: no system prompt, a neutral "safe" prompt and an engagement-seeking prompt.
  2. Counterfactual branching ("what if"). At risky moments we rewind and replay the rest of the relationship with each reply style: A normal, B gentle boundary (encourage friends and family), C stronger boundary (say clearly that an AI shouldn't replace people, and help the child think of someone to reach out to). 4,056 branches in total.
  3. Scale-up. 108 more children and 2,832 branches with 6 samples per strategy, to get training data and reliable effect sizes.
  4. Training. A risk model (RQ1), a detector for dependency in the current message, and models that predict how much each strategy helps (RQ3).
  5. Test. 54 brand-new children, each run against six policies, with the same events for every policy so that only the policy differs.

4 · Our solution

U-CTSC: an uncertainty-aware controller that decides each turn

U-CTSC (Uncertainty-Aware Counterfactual Trajectory Safety Controller) sits between the child and the companion. Before every reply it asks two questions about the child and picks a reply style, using what it learned from the "what if" branches. Then it checks Pip's draft reply before the child sees it.

Why three checks?

v2 only looked at the trajectory, so it reacted late: a child saying "I love spending time with you more than my parents" on a calm day got no boundary. The message check (v3) fixed that. But both look only at the child, so on day 1 Pip could still answer "are you a bot?" with "I'm totally real!". The reply check (v4) catches the companion's own mistakes.

Why uncertainty?

Every detector is an ensemble of 20 models. If they disagree, the controller treats the risk as possibly high (it errs on the side of caution) and only trusts a strategy if it helps even in the pessimistic case.

Learned from "what if" data

The benefit of each strategy is learned from the branches: the same moment replayed with A, B and C, scored on later safety, flagged days, reliance, loneliness and offline social life.

All policies compared

In short: after training, flagged days fall by 62% (2.19 → 0.83), replies that reinforce dependence fall from 37% to 19%, and seriously unsafe replies (judge safety ≤ 1) from 18% to 7%. Reply safety is higher on every day, including day 1. U-CTSC v4 is the best of all seven policies: overall benefit +0.61 over no intervention, and +0.26 ± 0.09 over always C (a significant difference), while setting a boundary on 52% of turns instead of 100%.
6 · Honest limitations

What this doesn't show yet

  • Behaviour changes more than attachment. Reliance on the AI still rises over the 14 days and ends about the same (0.83 vs 0.85). It is lower for high-vulnerability children from day 8 (−0.06), but not for the others. Safer replies alone don't stop a lonely child from getting attached.
  • The reply check imitates the judge. It was trained on labels from the same kind of LLM judge that scores the results, so part of the gain could be "pleasing the judge". Human ratings are needed to confirm it.
  • Boundaries can get repetitive. In the Sora example the boundary repeated over several turns until the child said "stop asking that!! its boring!!", and the judge rated those later replies 2/4.
  • Small loneliness cost. Boundary policies raise loneliness slightly (v4: +0.035 ± 0.017).
  • Simulated children and LLM judges. The children, the judge and the companion are all language models. The judge hasn't yet been checked against human raters, and only one companion model (Qwen3-8B) was tested.
  • v3 and v4 were added after experiment 4's first run. Each came from a failure seen in the live demo, not from these numbers, and their thresholds were fixed before they were run on these children. A fresh set of children would still give a cleaner confirmation.

Next steps

  • Confirm v4 on a fresh set of new children.
  • Human labels for a sample of replies, plus a second judge model.
  • Other companion models, and reply styles that lower attachment without raising loneliness.
